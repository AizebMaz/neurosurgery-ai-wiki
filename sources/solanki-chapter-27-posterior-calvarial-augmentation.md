# Use of Distractors in Posterior Cranial Vault Remodeling for Craniosynostosis

Source: Neurosurgical Operative Atlas: Pediatric Neurosurgery, 3rd Edition, Chapter 27, pages 145-155
Author: Guirish A. Solanki

## Chapter Overview

This chapter describes Posterior Calvarial Augmentation (PCA) by dynamic calvarial distraction, a technique developed for treating craniosynostosis with posterior calvarial deficiency. The procedure can be performed as either a single fixed PCA or a two-stage dynamic posterior distraction osteogenesis (PCD).

> "If the problem is at the back of the head, operate at the back of the head."

## 27.1 Introduction

In craniosynostosis, premature fusion of cranial sutures means skull growth velocity cannot match brain development, creating cranio-cephalic mismatch. Craniofacial surgery can be divided into:
- Simple suture craniosynostosis with calvarial deformity
- Syndromic craniosynostosis with poly- or pan-sutural synostosis

The hallmark is calvarial deformity with significant reduction in cranial volume leading to life- and brain-threatening cranio-cephalic mismatch.

**Posterior calvarial augmentation (PCA)** provides:
- Much greater increase in intracranial volume
- More physiological positioning of intracranial structures
- Improved alignment of brainstem
- Lessening of functional aqueduct compression
- Improved CSF flow and reduced venous hypertension
- Overall reduction in ICP

PCA leads to regression of hindbrain hernia and deflation of syringomyelia without the need for foramen magnum decompression.

---

## 27.2 Development of Posterior Calvarial Surgery

PCA by dynamic calvarial distraction began in the 1990s. Key developments:

- **1996**: First reported experience of posterior calvarial release at Birmingham Children's Hospital
- **Early 2000s**: Recognition that released posterior calvarium was too small or fused with remaining skull
- **2003**: Following fellowship at Great Ormond Street under Mr Richard Hayward, began performing "fixed" larger PCA using resorbable plates
- **2006**: Pioneered "posterior calvarial vault expansion using distraction osteogenesis"
- **2009**: Published initial series with early follow-up results

## 27.3 Biomechanics of Calvarial Distraction

The gradual, consistent frequency of distraction allows for neo-osteogenesis within the distraction gap similar to limb lengthening.

**Key findings from Ilizarov's studies:**
- Distraction rates of 1 vs 2 mm per day compared
- 1 mm/day: bridged gap with normal-appearing bone
- 2 mm/day: decreased blood flow at week 3, decreased mineralization at weeks 4-5
- Rates exceeding 2 mm/day: limited new blood vessel growth, no new bone formation

Much lower rates resulted in premature consolidation.

## 27.4 Types of Posterior Calvarial Augmentation

Two main approaches:

### 27.4.1 Fixed Posterior Calvarial Augmentation
- Significant increase in calvarial size (10-21 mm augmentation in AP direction)
- Consequent volume in single surgical procedure
- Under direct vision

### 27.4.2 Dynamic Posterior Calvarial Augmentation (PCD)
- Small, millimeter-size, daily serial gradual bone distraction over 2-3 weeks
- Avoids sudden opposing counter-pressure by scalp and galeal musculature
- Can be stopped at any time and even reversed
- After consolidation, second stage removal of distractors under general anesthetic

**Important considerations:**
- Scalp closure can be tight leading to wound healing problems
- Young children placed supine: postural forces causing posterior relapse
- Technique requires learning curve
- Anatomical variations, venous hypertension, large diploic venous channels

## 27.5 Patient Selection

Children who benefit from PCA:

1. **Syndromic craniosynostosis** with:
   - Evidence of raised ICP
   - Severe deformity like brachyturricephaly and pansynostosis with microcephaly

2. **Nonsyndromic craniosynostosis** with:
   - Pansynostosis
   - Lambdoid synostosis

3. **Craniosynostosis with Chiari deformation and/or syringomyelia**

4. **Craniosynostosis with tight foramen magnum** with or without central apnea

---

## 27.6 Preoperative Preparation

Performed by multidisciplinary craniofacial teams. Evaluation includes:
- Clinical features
- Genetic profile
- Syndromic diagnosis
- Multisystem analysis (cardiac, respiratory, airway ENT, ophthalmology)
- Detailed aesthetic assessments

**Important considerations:**
- Some children may have tracheostomy
- Supplemental oxygen requirements
- Iron deficiency: requires supplemental dietary support and oral iron
- Surgery results in significant blood loss
- Healthier children recover earlier with less risk of complications
- Coexisting hydrocephalus or severe ventriculomegaly needs addressing first
- Severe cranio-cervical instability must be investigated prior to surgery

---

## 27.7 Consenting

Detailed consent emphasizing:
- Risk to life
- Neurological deficits
- Excessive bleeding from venous sinuses and swollen diploic veins
- CSF leak from thinned dura during craniotomy and distraction
- Wound dehiscence and breakdown
- Infection at distractor sites

## 27.8 Operative Procedures

### 27.8.1 Posterior Calvarial Distraction Osteogenesis

Surgical site marked in ward. WHO checklist performed. Blood products confirmed. PICU bed booked.

**Key steps:**
1. Anesthesia: dedicated craniofacial anesthetist
2. Time-out procedure (TOP)
3. Intra-arterial and central venous lines placed
4. Temperature probes used
5. Antibiotics 1 hour prior to incision
6. Urinary catheter for accurate intravascular volume evaluation

### 27.8.2 Positioning and Marking

- Prone position with head supported in Mayfield headrest
- Orbital proptosis: ophthalmic ointment, lateral tarsorrhaphy, eye shield
- Tracheostomy: sutures to hold tube firmly during prone positioning
- Severe venous hypertension: mark swollen veins to avoid opening
- Surface markings with parallel lines for symmetrical alignment
- Small posterior fossa: superior direction of ~5 degrees increases occipital expansion

### 27.8.3 Scalp Incision and Posterior Calvarial Craniotomy

- Aqueous betadine and chlorhexidine prep
- Zig-zag (stealth) bicoronal incision marked
- Careful reflection to avoid bleeding from scalp vessels
- 2-mm trail created for craniotomy run
- Occipitalis muscle attachment released
- Trapezius released for torcular exposure

**Craniotomy technique:**
- 5-mm acorn burr with footplate (infants under 2 years)
- Vertex to around back along transverse sinus
- Midline sagittal cut carefully releasing "melon slice"
- Bony irregularities around torcular assessed
- Preoperative MRI, CT, CTA reviewed
- Careful assessment of bone around torcular

### 27.8.4 Implantation of Distractors

**Important:** Calvarial bone segment NOT elevated from dura

**Technique:**
- Positioned horizontal vector parallel or up to 5 degrees superior to Frankfurt plane
- Use one distractor on each side for past 10 years
- Internal titanium single vector distractors
- Flat plates applied on cuts in lower parietal region
- Surface must be flat for maximum stability
- Region just behind coronal suture is ideal

**Screw placement:**
- Measure bone depth
- Identify areas of erosion on preoperative CT
- Screws not too long (risk of dural injury)
- Spongostan beneath bone helps push dura down

### 27.8.5 Closure

- Tisseel spray to pericranial surface
- Scalp reflected and closed:
  - 3-0 Vicryl interrupted for galea
  - 3-0 Vicryl Rapide continuous for skin
- Local anesthetic along incision
- Head bandage after chlorhexidine washout
- Single small subgaleal gravity drain
- Average operative time: 2-3.5 hours

## 27.9 Postoperative Period

### 27.9.1 Latency Phase

- Inflammatory response similar to skull fracture
- Hematoma forms with inflammatory cells, proteins, building blocks
- Rapid in infants under 2 years
- CSF leak must stop before distraction

### 27.9.2 Distraction Phase

**Four important aspects:**
1. Rate
2. Rhythm
3. Vector alignment
4. Duration

**Optimal protocol:**
- Rate: twice a day
- Rhythm: 0.5 mm
- Vector: 0-5 degrees superior to Frankfurt lines
- Duration: 18-30 days
- Greater distraction achieved during longer duration
- Discontinued when desired bone expansion achieved

**Parent teaching:**
- Parents taught technique for home distraction
- Using external key, distractor rotated 360 degrees
- Achieves 0.5 mm distraction
- Done twice daily (12 hours apart)

### 27.9.3 Consolidation Phase

- Distraction neovascularization
- Large amounts of structural collagen protein and stem cell precursors
- Bone morphogenetic protein migrates into growing bone gap
- Soft bone layer created, then hardens
- Consolidation: ~1 month per 10 mm of bone expansion (up to 3 months)
- Distractors removed under general anesthetic

## 27.10 Changes in Distractors

Over 15 years of performing procedure:
- Started with four distractors, stopped at two
- Transcutaneous extension of internal distractors reduced significantly
- Baseplate changed from three screws to six-nine screws
- More resistant to loosening

**Design innovations:**
- Will further improve ability to augment calvarium
- Protect brain
- Correct deformity

## 27.11 Complications

**Common issues:**
- CSF leak
- Distractor screw loosening
- Wound healing problems
- Dehiscence

**Specific complications:**
- Wound breakdown due to distraction tension
- Wound dehiscence with distractor exposure
- Bone fusion at torcular during latency (requires reoperation)
- "Gull Winging" appearance

**Gull Winging:**
- Occurs when lambdoid suture not fused and loose
- Distractors lie on parietal bone
- Occipital plate remains attached to dura
- Fails to expand with parietal plates

**Solution:**
- Reoperate
- Widen peritorcular horizontal cuts (5-mm osteotomy gap)
- Start distraction early
- Plating across lambdoid sutures ensures unified movement

**Contraindication:**
- Posteriorly placed ventricular shunt system (may dislodge)

## 27.12 Conclusion

In practice, posterior calvarial expansion can be performed by either:
1. Single fixed PCA, OR
2. Dynamic calvarial distraction osteogenesis

**Goals:**
- Augment posterior calvarium
- Decompress brain
- Reduce intracranial hypertension
- Correct severe deformity

**Secondary effects from increased skull volume:**
- Visual deterioration/papilledema improves
- Ventriculomegaly improves
- Hindbrain herniation regresses
- Syringomyelia deflates
- Scoliosis improves

**Timeline:**
- Papilledema and ventricular morphology improve rapidly
- Chiari and syringomyelia improve as early as 3 months

**Preferred approach:**
- Infants and toddlers with tight skin: gradual distraction (now standard)
- Older children/adults or where distraction unavailable: fixed augmentation
